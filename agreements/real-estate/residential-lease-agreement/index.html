Residential lease agreement

A residential lease agreement gives a tenant the exclusive right to live in a specific property for a fixed term at a fixed rent. It is the standard document for a one-year tenancy, and it is the one document in a rental relationship that everything else refers back to.

What is a residential lease agreement?

A residential lease agreement is a contract in which a property owner gives one or more named tenants exclusive possession of a specific home for a fixed period, in exchange for rent. Exclusive possession is the part that matters: for the length of the term the tenant, not the owner, controls who comes through the door, subject only to the landlord's limited right of entry.

Because the term is fixed, both sides are committed for its length. The tenant owes rent for the whole term even if they move out early, and the landlord cannot raise the rent or end the tenancy inside it except for a breach the lease or the law allows. That mutual lock is the entire reason to choose a lease over a month-to-month agreement.

It is a property document, not just a contract

A lease transfers an interest in real property, which is why it survives a sale. If the landlord sells the building mid-term, the buyer takes it with the lease attached and the tenant stays on the same terms until the term ends.

When a lease is the right choice, and when it is not

Choose a fixed-term lease when both sides want certainty: the tenant wants a settled home and a rent that cannot move, and the landlord wants a full year of income without re-advertising. Choose something else when either side genuinely might need out.

Which clauses identify the deal

A workable residential lease has about fourteen substantive clauses. Four of them identify the deal, five govern the money, and five govern living in the property. Anything beyond those is either a required state disclosure or a clause solving a problem specific to your property. The first four are the ones a court reads to work out what was agreed at all.

  • Parties. Every adult occupant named as a tenant, and the landlord named as whoever legally owns or manages the property. An adult who lives there but is not on the lease is not liable for the rent.
  • Property. The full address with unit number, and an explicit list of what is included: parking space number, storage locker, appliances, yard, basement.
  • Term. Start date and end date, stated as dates and not as “twelve months”. Add what happens if the tenant stays past the end date, or you inherit a holdover tenancy on whatever terms the state supplies.
  • Occupancy. Who may live there, and how long a guest can stay before they count as an occupant. This is the clause that prevents a two-person tenancy quietly becoming a five-person one.

Which clauses govern the money

Five clauses carry the money, and each one exists because of a dispute that recurs. State every figure as a figure and every date as a date: a lease that describes the rent as due at the beginning of the month has not said whether the third is late.

  • Rent. The amount, the due date, and whether there is a grace period. State the payment method, because “I paid cash” is the single hardest dispute to resolve.
  • Late fees. The amount and when it triggers. Many states cap late fees as a percentage of rent or require a minimum grace period, and an uncapped fee is often unenforceable.
  • Security deposit. The amount, what it may be used for, where it is held, and the deadline for returning it. See security deposit for what the law generally allows.
  • Utilities. Which party pays which service, named individually. “Tenant pays utilities” leaves water, trash and sewer genuinely ambiguous in most markets.
  • Returned payment fee. What happens when a payment bounces, subject to any state cap.

Which clauses govern living in the property

The remaining five govern the year itself, and they are the ones actually argued about. A lease can be complete on parties, rent and term and still produce a dispute every month if it is silent on repairs, entry and what may be hung on a wall.

  • Maintenance and repairs. What the tenant handles, what the landlord handles, and how a repair is requested. Put the request method in writing so there is a record of when you were told.
  • Landlord entry. Notice required before entry and the permitted reasons. Most states require advance written notice except in emergencies, commonly twenty-four hours, and this clause is what keeps a landlord on the right side of quiet enjoyment.
  • Pets. Allowed or not, which animals, any pet rent or deposit. Note that assistance animals are handled under fair-housing law and are not pets for this purpose.
  • Alterations. Whether the tenant may paint, mount a television, change locks. Silence here produces the most common deposit argument there is.
  • Condition and return. The property is returned in the condition received, less normal wear and tear. Attach a signed move-in condition report, because without one this clause has nothing to compare against.

Clauses that will not hold up

Some clauses appear in circulating templates and are unenforceable in most or all states. Signing them does not make them valid, and including them can expose a landlord to a penalty in the states that treat them as a violation in themselves.

Commonly struck out
  • Waiver of habitability. A tenant cannot agree that the landlord need not keep the property liveable. The implied warranty of habitability is not waivable in most states.
  • Self-help eviction. A clause allowing the landlord to change the locks, remove belongings or shut off utilities to force a tenant out. Illegal in essentially every state, and often carries damages.
  • Waiver of notice or court process. A tenant cannot agree in advance to be removed without the statutory notice and court procedure.
  • Non-refundable “deposits”. In many states a deposit is refundable by definition, and calling a fee a deposit does not change what it is.
  • Blanket liability waivers. A clause absolving the landlord of responsibility for their own negligence is void in a large number of states.

A lease is not weakened by leaving these out. It is weakened by including them, because a court that strikes one clause reads the rest of the document more sceptically.

What to do before the tenant moves in

  1. Screen and document the decisionRun the rental application with written consent, and keep the same criteria for every applicant. If you decline someone because of a consumer report, federal law requires you to tell them so and name the agency.
  2. Fill the lease with your actual numbersRent, deposit, term dates, utilities, pets. Every blank left as a placeholder is a term nobody agreed to.
  3. Attach the required disclosuresLead-based paint for pre-1978 housing, plus whatever your state and city require. Attachments are part of the lease and should be signed too.
  4. Record the condition, with dates on the photographsWalk the property with the tenant, fill in a condition report room by room, and both sign it. This is the document that decides the deposit argument eleven months from now.
  5. Sign, and hand over a complete copyBoth sides sign; the tenant leaves with the lease, the attachments and the condition report. Then keys.
The move-in report is the highest-value ten minutes

Almost every contested deposit comes down to the condition of the place on day one. Ten minutes and forty photographs at move-in settle it. Nothing you can write into the lease substitutes for it.

What a lease template cannot decide for you

A template cannot tell you your state's deposit cap, its deposit return deadline, its required entry notice, or whether your city has rent stabilisation. Those are the four things that vary most and they are exactly the four that a generic template leaves blank or fills wrongly.

It also cannot tell you whether your property is legally rentable: short-term rental rules, occupancy limits, licensing or registration requirements and habitability inspections are all local, and a signed lease for an unlicensed unit can be unenforceable for the landlord in some cities.

And it does not decide fair-housing questions. Screening criteria, occupancy limits and pet rules all have to sit inside federal fair-housing law and whatever additional protected classes your state and city add.

Common mistakes

A fixed-term lease usually fails on what was left out of it rather than on the clauses that made it in.

  • Leaving the end date to be settled laterA fixed term needs both a start and an end. Without both you have created a periodic tenancy by accident, and the notice rules that govern it are not the ones you had in mind.
  • Copying a clause you cannot explainIf you cannot say what a clause protects against, you cannot argue it. Clauses purporting to waive a tenant's statutory rights are generally void, and arriving with several of them does not help the clauses that were sound.
  • Not naming every adult who will live thereSomebody who is not on the lease owes you nothing under it, and in several states is still difficult to remove. See joint and several liability.
  • Signing before any condition record existsThe move-in record is the baseline every later deduction is measured against. Made afterwards, it proves nothing. See a new tenant moving in.
  • Treating the deposit clause as free textThe cap, the account it sits in and the return deadline are set by state law. A lease cannot contract out of any of them. See security deposit.

Red flags

What to look for in a lease somebody else drafted and is waiting for you to sign.

  • “Tenant accepts the property as is”Paired with no condition report, that sentence is doing its work at move-out rather than at move-in.
  • Entry at any time, or at the landlord's discretionMost states require advance written notice except in an emergency, and the clause does not override the statute. See quiet enjoyment.
  • A waiver of the right to sue, or to a juryWidely unenforceable in residential tenancies, and a reliable sign the document was not drafted for residential use.
  • Automatic renewal buried in the term clauseA term that rolls into another fixed year unless you give notice months ahead is a commitment you are making now, not later. See lease renewal.
  • The deposit held in the landlord's general accountSeveral states require a separate or interest-bearing account, and a written notice of where the money sits.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a residential lease be?

Twelve months is the American default, and it is the default because it matches the rhythm of the rental market: you re-advertise in the same season you let last time. Six months suits an uncertain tenant or an untested property; two years suits a tenant you want to keep and a market you expect to soften. What matters more than the length is that the end date lands in a month when your area actually has renters looking.

Can I raise the rent during a fixed-term lease?

No, not unless the lease itself contains a clause providing for it, which is unusual in a one-year residential lease. A fixed term means a fixed rent for that term. You can raise the rent at renewal, subject to any state notice requirement and any local rent-stabilisation rule, using a lease renewal agreement.

What happens if the tenant moves out before the term ends?

The tenant remains liable for the rent for the rest of the term, but in most states the landlord has a duty to mitigate: they must make reasonable efforts to re-rent the property rather than let it sit empty and bill the departed tenant for the whole balance. The cleaner route for both sides is a lease termination agreement that states exactly what is owed and releases both parties.

Signing, deposits and who is liable

Do all the adults living there have to sign?

They should. Every adult who signs is jointly and severally liable, which means you can pursue any one of them for the whole rent. See joint and several liability. An adult living there who did not sign owes you nothing and, in some states, is still hard to remove without a court process.

Is an electronically signed lease valid?

Yes. Under the federal E-SIGN Act and the state versions of UETA, an electronic signature on a residential lease is generally as valid as an ink one, and courts routinely enforce them. Keep the audit trail showing who signed, when and from where, since that is what proves it if it is ever questioned.

Can I charge whatever security deposit I want?

Not everywhere. Many states cap the deposit at one or two months' rent, several cap it only for certain property types, and some have no statutory cap at all. Several states also regulate where the money is held and whether interest is owed. Check your state before you set the number.
