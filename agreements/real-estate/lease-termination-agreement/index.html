Lease termination agreement

A lease termination agreement ends a tenancy early by mutual consent. Nobody is in breach, both sides are released, and the money — remaining rent, the deposit, any fee — is settled in the document rather than argued about afterwards.

What a lease termination agreement is

A lease termination agreement — also called a mutual termination, a surrender agreement or a lease cancellation — is a contract in which landlord and tenant agree to end a fixed-term lease before its end date and release each other from the obligations that would otherwise continue. It replaces the lease's remaining term with an agreed exit.

What makes it worth signing rather than handling informally is the release. Without one, a tenant who moves out early is still liable for the remaining rent, and a landlord who lets them go is still holding a deposit against a tenancy that has no agreed end. The agreement converts an open-ended exposure on both sides into a settled figure and a date.

What the agreement has to settle

Five things, and a termination agreement that leaves any of them out will be argued about later: the date possession ends, what is still owed, what happens to the deposit, the mutual release, and the condition the property is returned in.

ItemWhat to state
Termination dateThe exact date the tenancy ends and keys are returned. Rent is owed up to it and not beyond.
Final paymentThe precise sum the tenant pays, if any: unpaid rent, an agreed early-termination fee, re-letting costs. A number, not a formula.
Security depositWhether it is returned, applied to the final sum, or held pending the usual inspection and statutory deadline. Say which.
Mutual releaseEach side releases the other from all further claims under the lease, from the termination date onward.
Condition and keysThe property is returned broom-clean less normal wear and tear, with all keys, fobs and openers, and a joint inspection at handover.
Do not release the deposit obligation by accident

A broad mutual release signed before the move-out inspection can be read as releasing the landlord's statutory duty to itemise and return the deposit — or as releasing the tenant from damage that has not been found yet. If the deposit is to be dealt with afterwards under the normal statutory process, carve it out of the release expressly.

When you do not need one

You do not need a mutual termination when the tenant has a statutory right to leave. Most states give tenants an unconditional right to end a lease early in defined circumstances, and in those cases the tenant serves the required notice and documentation and the lease ends by law.

  • Active-duty military. The federal Servicemembers Civil Relief Act permits a servicemember to terminate a residential lease on qualifying orders, with written notice and a copy of the orders.
  • Domestic violence. Most states allow a survivor to end a tenancy early on specified documentation, and prohibit penalising them for it.
  • Uninhabitable property. Where a landlord has failed to repair a condition affecting habitability after proper notice, many states permit the tenant to treat the lease as ended. The procedural requirements are strict and vary widely.
  • A clause in the lease. Some leases contain their own early-termination option, typically requiring notice plus a fee of one or two months' rent. If yours does, use it rather than negotiating a new agreement.

You also do not need one at the natural end of a term. That is a non-renewal, handled with a notice to vacate or simply by the term expiring.

What an early termination normally costs

There is no standard figure, because what a tenant owes on an early exit is whatever the parties agree — and the landlord's alternative is not the full remaining rent. In most states a landlord has a duty to mitigate: they must make reasonable efforts to re-rent, and the tenant is liable only for rent lost until a new tenant is found, plus reasonable re-letting costs.

That duty is the negotiating frame for both sides. A tenant leaving in a strong rental market with four months left is realistically exposed to a few weeks of vacancy and an advertising cost, not four months of rent. A tenant leaving in a weak market in December is exposed to more. One or two months' rent as an agreed fee is a common landing point, and it is common precisely because it approximates what a vacancy actually costs.

Both sides gain certainty

A negotiated figure signed today is worth more to a landlord than a larger figure they would have to sue for, and worth more to a tenant than an unknown exposure hanging over them. That is the trade the document exists to record.

Frequently asked questions

Can my landlord refuse to let me out of my lease early?

Yes, unless you have a statutory or contractual right to terminate. A fixed-term lease binds both sides, and a landlord is entitled to hold you to it. In practice most will negotiate, because the alternative is chasing an absent tenant for rent while the property sits empty — and their duty to mitigate means they have to try to re-rent it anyway.

Do I still get my deposit back if we terminate early?

Yes. Ending the lease early does not forfeit the deposit. It is still held against unpaid rent and damage beyond normal wear and tear, and the statutory deadline for returning it with an itemised statement runs from the date the tenancy ends and possession is returned. Make sure the termination agreement says whether the deposit is being applied to the final sum or returned separately.

What if the landlord wants me out early because they are selling?

A sale does not end a lease. A buyer generally takes the property subject to the existing tenancy and the tenant stays on the same terms until the term ends. If the landlord or buyer wants possession sooner, that is a negotiation, and the tenant is entitled to be compensated for agreeing — this is the situation that a mutual termination agreement, with a payment running the other way, is made for.

Is a verbal agreement to end the lease enough?

It is risky in both directions. Without writing, a tenant has no proof they were released and can be billed for months of rent, and a landlord has no proof of the date possession was surrendered or what was agreed about the deposit. The document is short. The dispute it prevents is not.
