Residential rental agreements for US landlords and tenants

Nine documents cover almost every residential tenancy in the United States: one to advertise and screen, one to create the tenancy, three to change it, and two to end it. This page tells you which of the nine you are actually looking for before you spend time on the wrong one.

Which rental document do you need?

If you are handing over a home for a fixed period at a fixed rent, you need a residential lease agreement. If you want the arrangement to renew every month and be endable on short notice, you need a month-to-month rental agreement. Everything else in this category either happens before the tenancy starts, changes it while it runs, or ends it.

Lease or rental agreement — is there a difference?

In everyday American usage the two words are interchangeable, but in the documents they are not. A lease runs for a fixed term and neither side can walk away mid-term without consequences. A rental agreement usually means a periodic tenancy, most often month-to-month, that renews on its own until somebody gives notice.

The practical difference is who carries the risk of change. Under a fixed-term lease the tenant is committed to paying for the whole term and the landlord cannot raise the rent inside it. Under a month-to-month agreement either side can walk with proper notice, and rent can be changed with notice, which suits a landlord who may want to sell or move back in and a tenant who is not sure how long they are staying.

Both are binding

A month-to-month agreement is not a weaker contract. It is a contract with a shorter renewal cycle. A tenant on month-to-month has the same habitability rights, the same deposit protections and the same protection from being locked out as a tenant on a twelve-month lease.

What every residential rental document has to contain

Whatever form it takes, a residential rental document has to identify the parties, identify the property, state the money and state the term. A document missing any of those four is not enforceable as written, because a court reading it cannot tell who owes what to whom.

The parties

Every adult who will live there, by legal name, and the landlord as the entity that actually owns or manages the property.

The property

Full street address including unit number, plus what comes with it: parking space, storage, appliances, yard.

The money

Rent amount, due date, how it is paid, any late fee and when it applies, and the security deposit.

The term

Start date, end date or renewal cycle, and what happens when the term runs out.

Beyond those four, the clauses that actually get argued about later are the ones covering repairs, entry, pets, guests, and what condition the place has to be returned in. A document that is silent on those does not make the disputes go away; it makes them turn on whose memory a judge finds more credible.

Disclosures federal and state law require

Federal law requires one disclosure in nearly all residential rentals: for housing built before 1978, the landlord must disclose known lead-based paint hazards, give the tenant the EPA-approved pamphlet, and include a lead warning statement in the lease. States and cities add their own on top.

Common state-level additions include naming the person authorised to receive notices, disclosing where the security deposit is held, disclosing known mould or flooding history, and notifying the tenant of a pending foreclosure. Several cities require a notice of local rent-stabilisation rules to be attached to every lease.

The disclosure is not optional paperwork

Missing a required disclosure can carry a statutory penalty independent of anything else, and in some states it gives the tenant a defence in an eviction action. Check what your state and city require before you use any template, including this one.

What these documents do not do

A rental document sets the terms between the two sides. It does not override the law, and it cannot be used to sign away rights the law gives the tenant. Clauses that waive the implied warranty of habitability, waive the right to a court process before eviction, or impose penalties a statute caps are commonly struck out by courts even when both parties signed.

None of these documents evict anybody. Ending a tenancy against a tenant's will is a court process with its own rules in every state, and a notice to vacate is only the first step in it.

And none of them replace state law on the amount or handling of deposits, required notice periods, or the landlord's repair obligations. Where a document and a statute disagree, the statute wins.

Common mistakes

This page routes rather than drafts, so the errors that belong to it are errors of choosing — picking a document by the word people use for it rather than by what the tenancy actually is.

  • Choosing by the name instead of the term“Lease” and “rental agreement” are used interchangeably in ordinary speech. What decides which document you need is whether the tenancy runs for a fixed period or renews by the month — not which of the two words sounds more official.
  • Taking a generic US template as finishedA template with no state on it cannot carry the disclosures your state requires. The agreement may be perfectly enforceable on its own terms and still leave you in breach of a rule that sits outside it.
  • Making one document do two jobsA lease that also tries to be a roommate agreement or a sublease usually does neither well. Each document here exists because it settles something none of the others settle.
  • Leaving it undocumented because it is familyAn oral tenancy is still a tenancy in most states, and it still has to be ended lawfully. Informality removes the evidence, not the obligations.

Red flags

What to notice about a rental document somebody has handed you, before you get as far as reading the clauses.

  • No state named anywhere in itResidential tenancy is state and often city law. A document that never names a jurisdiction was not written for yours.
  • Deposit terms left blank to fill in laterMost states cap the amount, regulate where it is held and set a deadline for its return. A blank is where those rules get broken. See security deposit.
  • The other side wants to skip the written agreementWhoever proposes the handshake is usually the party who benefits from there being no record of what was agreed.
  • The parties are not named precisely“The tenant” with no full legal name, or a company that appears in no register, is a party you may not be able to pursue.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a written lease, or is a verbal agreement enough?

Most states will recognise a verbal residential tenancy of a year or less, and it usually defaults to month-to-month. But a tenancy for longer than a year generally has to be in writing to be enforceable under the state's statute of frauds, and even a short verbal tenancy leaves you with no record of the rent, the deposit or the condition of the property. Written is not a formality here; it is the only version anyone can prove.

Can I use the same lease for every state I own property in?

No. The body of the agreement travels well, but the disclosures, the deposit handling rules, the notice periods and the late-fee limits are all state law, and several of them are also city law. Use one structure and swap the state-specific parts, rather than one file everywhere.

Does the landlord have to sign, or is the tenant's signature enough?

Both sides sign. A lease signed by only the tenant is an offer the landlord has not accepted, and it leaves the tenant with no signed promise that the rent is what they were told. Each side should end up holding a fully signed copy.

What if my tenant is a company renting for an employee?

That is a corporate tenancy, and the document changes: the company is the tenant and is liable for the rent, while the person living there is named as the permitted occupant. Do not simply put the employee's name on a standard residential lease, because then the company is not on the hook for anything.
