Notice to vacate

A notice to vacate is a one-sided declaration that a tenancy is ending. It is not an agreement and needs nobody's signature but the sender's. What it does need is the right notice period, the right delivery method, and the right date — because a defective notice is worth nothing.

What a notice to vacate is

A notice to vacate is a written statement from one party to the other that the tenancy will end on a stated date. A tenant serves it to say they are leaving. A landlord serves it to end a periodic tenancy, to decline to renew a fixed term, or to begin the process of removing a tenant who has breached the lease.

It is unilateral. The other side does not agree to it and does not sign it. That is the difference between a notice and a termination agreement, and it is why the formalities matter so much: with no counter-signature to prove agreement, the only thing establishing that the notice was valid is that it complied with the rules.

Names differ by state

The same document appears as a notice to quit, a notice of non-renewal, a notice of termination or a notice to vacate depending on the jurisdiction and the reason. A notice to quit for non-payment is a particular species with its own short statutory period, usually between three and fourteen days.

How much notice, and by when

For a month-to-month tenancy, thirty days is the common baseline in most states, frequently required to expire at the end of a rental period, and frequently longer — sixty days or more — when it is the landlord giving notice to a tenant of a year or more. For a fixed-term lease that is simply ending, the lease itself usually sets the notice, often thirty or sixty days before the end date.

These are the common patterns, not your state's law

Notice periods are among the most variable rules in American landlord-tenant law, and several states prescribe the exact wording and even the typeface of a notice to quit. Verify your state and city before serving anything you intend to rely on in court.

What a valid notice contains

  • The date it was written and served. The clock runs from service, not from writing.
  • The names of both parties and the full property address, including unit number.
  • The date the tenancy ends. A specific date, not “in thirty days”. The recipient should not have to do arithmetic to know when to be out.
  • The reason, where one is required. Just-cause jurisdictions require a permitted ground to be stated, and a notice for non-payment must usually state the amount owed and the period it covers.
  • What happens next. For a tenant giving notice: the forwarding address for the deposit. For a landlord: where and how to return keys, and that a move-out inspection will be offered where the state requires it.
  • The sender's signature and contact details.

Delivery is part of validity

Most states prescribe how a notice may be served: personal delivery, leaving it with an adult occupant and mailing a copy, posting it conspicuously and mailing a copy, or certified mail. Email or text is increasingly accepted between the parties for a tenant's own notice, but it is rarely sufficient for a landlord's notice that may end up before a judge. Serve it the way the statute allows and keep proof.

What a notice to vacate does not do

It does not remove anybody. If the tenant does not leave by the stated date, the landlord's next step is to file an eviction action in court — variously called unlawful detainer, summary process or forcible entry and detainer — and obtain a judgment and a writ. Only a sheriff or marshal executes it.

Self-help is unlawful everywhere

Changing the locks, removing the tenant's belongings, shutting off water, heat or electricity, or removing doors to force a tenant out is illegal in essentially every state, regardless of how much rent is owed or how valid the notice was. In many states it carries statutory damages several times the monthly rent, plus the tenant's legal costs — and it converts a case the landlord was going to win into one they will lose.

A notice also does not stop rent accruing. The tenant owes rent for the period they remain in possession, whether or not the notice has expired, and a landlord who accepts rent for a period after the notice date may in some states be treated as having created a new tenancy. If you are in that position, take the rent as use-and-occupancy and say so in writing, or do not take it.

Common mistakes

A notice does one job on a fixed clock, and most failures are procedural. A defective notice usually has to be served again from the beginning, which costs the whole period over.

  • Counting the days wrongWhether the day of service counts, whether the period runs to the end of a rental period and whether weekends are included are all state questions, and getting any of them wrong invalidates the notice.
  • Serving it in a way the state does not recogniseSeveral states specify the permitted methods and require proof of service. A text message is rarely one of them.
  • Naming the wrong partiesEvery adult tenant generally has to be named. A notice that misses one may not end the tenancy for any of them.
  • Treating the notice as the evictionIt ends a tenancy or starts a clock. It removes nobody — only a court order does that. See when a tenant stops paying rent.
  • Accepting rent after serving itIn many states that waives the notice and can create a fresh tenancy. See holdover tenant.

Red flags

What to check on a notice that has been served on you.

  • No date of service anywhere on itWithout a service date the clock cannot be verified, and the notice may not have started one.
  • A demand to be out in 24 or 48 hoursNo ordinary residential tenancy in the United States ends on that timescale outside a court process.
  • A threat to change locks or cut utilitiesSelf-help eviction is unlawful in every state, and a notice threatening it tells you how the rest is likely to be handled.
  • Signed by somebody with no stated authorityAn agent serving notice should say whose agent they are. A signature with no capacity given is a gap worth raising.

Frequently asked questions

Can I give notice by text or email?

For a tenant telling a landlord they are leaving, many leases now permit it and most landlords accept it — but send it in the form the lease specifies as well, and keep a copy. For a landlord's notice that might need to support an eviction, follow the statutory service method exactly. A notice a judge will not accept is not a notice.

My landlord gave me notice but I haven't done anything wrong. Can they do that?

In most of the United States a landlord may end a month-to-month tenancy, or decline to renew a fixed term, without giving a reason, provided the notice period is right. The exceptions are significant and growing: just-cause jurisdictions require a permitted ground, and everywhere a notice given because of your race, religion, family status or disability, or in retaliation for a complaint about conditions, is unlawful regardless of the notice period.

I gave notice and then changed my mind. Can I withdraw it?

Only if the other side agrees. Once served, a valid notice ends the tenancy on the stated date as a matter of law, and the landlord may well have already advertised or signed a new tenant. Ask in writing straight away, and if they agree, record the withdrawal in writing too.

Does the notice period start the day I hand it over?

Usually the day after service, and in many states it must additionally expire at the end of a rental period, which can push the effective date well past the bare notice period. That is the single most common defect in notices served by landlords without advice. Count the days against your own state's method before you commit to a date.

What if the tenant leaves belongings behind?

Abandoned property has its own statutory procedure in most states: a notice to the former tenant, a storage period, and a prescribed method of disposal or sale. Do not simply dispose of it. The procedure is short and following it is far cheaper than the claim that follows not following it.
