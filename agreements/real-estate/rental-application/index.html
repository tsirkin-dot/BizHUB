Rental application

A rental application collects the facts a landlord needs to decide, and the written consent needed to verify them. It is the document that turns screening from a hunch into a process you can defend if the decision is ever questioned.

What a rental application is for

A rental application does two jobs at once. It gathers identity, income, rental history and references so the landlord can assess whether the applicant can pay and will look after the property. And it captures the applicant's signed authorisation to contact employers, previous landlords, and consumer reporting agencies — without which most of that verification is not lawful.

The second job is the one that gets skipped and the one that matters. Running a credit or background check on somebody is regulated under the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act, and the permissible purpose rests on the applicant's authorisation. That authorisation belongs on the application, in clear language, above the signature.

What a rental application should ask for

Identity

Full legal name, date of birth, current address, phone, email, and government identification for verification.

Everyone who will live there

All proposed adult occupants, each of whom applies separately, plus the number of children.

Income and employment

Employer, position, length of employment, gross monthly income, and how it will be documented.

Rental history

Two or three previous addresses with landlord names and contact details, and the reason for leaving each.

Pets and vehicles

Species, breed, weight and number for pets; make and plate for any vehicle needing a space.

Consent and signature

Authorisation to verify the information and obtain a consumer report, dated and signed.

Ask the same questions of everybody

The defence against a discrimination claim is a consistent, written process applied identically to every applicant. A landlord who uses one form, one set of criteria and one order of decision has a straightforward answer to why an applicant was declined. A landlord who improvises does not.

What you may not ask or use

The federal Fair Housing Act prohibits refusing to rent, or treating an applicant differently, because of race, colour, national origin, religion, sex, familial status or disability. Questions designed to reveal those characteristics are a problem even when the eventual decision would have been the same.

Questions to keep off the form
  • Whether the applicant has children, is pregnant, or plans to have children — familial status is protected.
  • Nationality, birthplace, native language, or immigration questions asked of some applicants and not others.
  • Religion, place of worship, or availability on particular days for religious reasons.
  • Disability, medical conditions, medication, or the nature of a service or assistance animal. You may ask whether an animal is required because of a disability; you may not ask what the disability is.
  • Marital status, sexual orientation or gender identity — protected federally as sex discrimination in housing, and expressly protected in many states.

Many states and cities add protected classes of their own, most commonly source of income — which makes refusing a housing voucher unlawful — along with age, marital status and military status. A growing number of jurisdictions also restrict when, or whether, criminal history may be considered, and several require that it not be asked until after a conditional approval.

Application fees and holding deposits

Most states allow a landlord to charge an application fee to cover the actual cost of screening, and many cap it, require it to be receipted, or require the unused portion to be returned. Several states prohibit charging it at all if no screening is carried out.

A holding deposit is a different thing and should be documented separately: it is money taken to take the property off the market while the applicant decides, and the written terms should say what happens to it if the applicant withdraws, if the landlord declines them, and whether it is credited against the security deposit on move-in.

Keep the two apart on paper. A single payment described as both is the kind of ambiguity that becomes a small-claims case.

Declining an applicant: the notice you owe

If you decline an applicant, raise their deposit, or require a co-signer wholly or partly because of information in a consumer report, the Fair Credit Reporting Act requires you to give the applicant an adverse action notice. It must tell them the report was used, identify the agency that supplied it, and state their right to a free copy and to dispute what it contains.

The notice is not an admission of anything and it does not require you to explain your reasoning. It is a disclosure that a report was involved and where it came from. Landlords who skip it usually do so because they think a polite verbal decline is kinder; it is also a breach, and it is one an applicant can pursue.

Keep the file

Keep the application, the criteria you applied, the report, the decision and the notice for each applicant, for as long as your state's limitations period runs. Consistency you cannot evidence is consistency you cannot rely on.

Common mistakes

Screening errors are mostly compliance errors, and almost all of them happen before anybody has been turned down.

  • Asking a question that maps to a protected classFamilial status and religion catch people who had no discriminatory intent whatsoever. Intent is not the test; the effect of the question is.
  • Running a check without written consentConsumer report checks require the applicant's documented permission. A verbal yes is not a record of one.
  • Charging a fee larger than the screening actually costsApplication fees are capped or prohibited in several states, and a fee that turns a profit is the version that attracts attention.
  • Declining without the notice you oweIf the decision rested on a consumer report, adverse action notice obligations apply. Silence is not a lawful decline.
  • Keeping no record of why each applicant was chosenConsistent written criteria applied to every applicant are what answer a complaint. Recollection is not.

Red flags

Signals worth a second question — every one about the paperwork, none about the applicant. Anything that maps to a protected class is not a red flag, it is a fair-housing problem of your own making.

  • Dates that do not line up between employer and previous landlordTwo references describing different years is a documentary inconsistency, and asking about it is fair.
  • A previous landlord reachable only on a number the applicant suppliedVerify the property against public records rather than relying on the contact given to you.
  • Cash up front offered in place of screeningSeveral months paid at once instead of any check is a proposal to remove your only record of who is moving in.
  • Consent withheld for a check explained in advanceThe refusal itself is the signal, and it is about the process rather than the person.

Frequently asked questions

Can I just ask for a credit report without a signed application?

No. A consumer reporting agency may supply a tenant screening report only for a permissible purpose, and in the rental context that rests on the applicant's written authorisation. Running a report without it exposes you to liability under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, and reputable screening services will not process the request in the first place.

How much income should I require?

A common benchmark in the United States is gross monthly income of about three times the rent, but it is a convention rather than a rule, and several cities now restrict rigid income multiples where a housing voucher covers part of the rent. Whatever threshold you choose, write it down before you advertise and apply it to every applicant identically.

Can I refuse someone because of a past eviction?

In most places, yes, and it is a conventional screening criterion. But a number of states and cities now limit how far back you may look, prohibit reliance on cases that were dismissed or sealed, or require an individualised assessment rather than a blanket rule. Check your jurisdiction, and apply whatever rule you adopt consistently.

Do I have to tell a rejected applicant why?

You are not obliged to explain your reasoning. You are obliged, if a consumer report contributed to the decision, to give an adverse action notice naming the agency and telling the applicant about their right to a copy and to dispute it. Those are two different things, and only the second is mandatory.

Is the application binding on either side?

No. An application is an offer to rent, not a tenancy. Nobody is committed until a lease is signed by both sides. A holding deposit can create limited obligations in the interval, which is why its terms should be written down separately.
