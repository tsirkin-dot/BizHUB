Roommate agreement

A roommate agreement governs the arrangements between people who share a home — who pays what, who cleans what, and what happens when one of them leaves. It is a contract among the tenants. The landlord is not a party to it and usually never sees it.

What is a roommate agreement?

A roommate agreement is a private contract between people living together in the same rented home. It divides rent, deposit, utilities, chores, guest rules and the process for somebody moving out. It sits alongside the lease, and it binds only the people who sign it.

It is worth writing for exactly the reason it feels awkward to write: everybody agrees about the rules while they are all getting along, and nobody agrees about them afterwards. The document is not a sign of distrust between friends. It is the thing that lets the friendship survive somebody's cat, somebody's partner moving in, and somebody leaving in April.

What a roommate agreement cannot do

It cannot change the lease. If all the roommates signed the lease, each of them is jointly and severally liable to the landlord for the entire rent, and a private agreement that everybody pays only their third does not bind the landlord at all. See joint and several liability.

The practical consequence is worth stating plainly. If one roommate stops paying, the landlord can demand the whole rent from any of the others, and will. The roommate agreement does not stop that. What it does is give the roommates who paid a written basis to recover from the one who did not.

It also cannot evict anybody

A roommate who signed the lease is the landlord's tenant, and the other roommates cannot remove them, change the locks or end their tenancy. Only the landlord can, through the court process. A roommate agreement can say somebody must leave; enforcing it is a separate matter.

What to put in it

TopicWhat to decideWhy it comes up
Rent splitExact dollar amount each person pays, and to whomRooms are rarely equal. Decide once, in writing, rather than every month.
Who pays the landlordOne person collecting and paying, or everyone paying separatelyA single payer is simpler and makes one person the bookkeeper. Say which.
DepositWho contributed what, and how it is divided at the endThe landlord returns one sum. Without a record, the split is argued from memory.
UtilitiesWhich account is in whose name, and how each bill is dividedThe named account holder takes the credit hit. That should be compensated or rotated.
Shared goodsWho owns the sofa, the router, the kitchen thingsEverything bought together has to be divided when somebody leaves.
GuestsHow many nights before a guest counts as an occupantMost lease-violation problems in shared houses start here.
Quiet hours and choresThe specific hours, and a rota if anybody wants oneUnwritten expectations differ more than anybody expects.
Leaving earlyNotice required, who finds the replacement, who approves themThe single most common failure point in shared tenancies.
DamageWho pays when one person causes itThe lease charges the tenants collectively. This clause allocates it internally.

The clause worth the most: somebody leaves early

The most valuable paragraph in a roommate agreement is the one covering a departure before the lease ends. It should state the notice required, who is responsible for finding a replacement, who has to approve them, what happens to the departing person's deposit share, and until when they keep paying if no replacement is found.

  1. NoticeA fixed period in writing to the other roommates. Thirty days matches most rent cycles and gives a realistic window to replace somebody.
  2. Finding a replacementName whose job it is. Usually the person leaving, since they are the one who wants out.
  3. ApprovalThe remaining roommates approve the replacement, and the landlord approves them too if the lease requires adding or substituting a tenant.
  4. MoneyThe departing roommate keeps paying until a replacement takes over, and is repaid their deposit share by the incoming roommate rather than by the landlord.
  5. PaperworkIf the landlord agrees, the lease is amended to swap the names. If not, the arrangement is a sublease and the departing person stays liable.

Common mistakes

A roommate agreement settles matters between tenants. Nearly every mistake is an attempt to make it settle something it has no power over.

  • Expecting it to bind the landlordThe landlord is not a party to it. However the rent is divided between you, the lease is what the landlord enforces. See joint and several liability.
  • Not writing down what happens when somebody leaves earlyThis is the clause worth the most and the one most often missing: who finds the replacement, who carries the rent until then, and what becomes of the leaver's share of the deposit.
  • Splitting the deposit informallyThe landlord returns one deposit to the tenants named on the lease. Any split between roommates has to be written down or it is argued about at the end.
  • Assuming equal rooms mean equal sharesUnequal rooms, parking or storage are exactly the things that become grievances later if the agreement does not name them now.

Red flags

Signals from a prospective roommate that the arrangement carries more risk than it appears to.

  • They will not go on the leaseSomeone off the lease owes the landlord nothing, which means any shortfall lands on the people who are on it.
  • No interest in anything writtenThe person least interested in a written split is usually the person who expects to benefit from there being none.
  • Already living there before anything is signedOccupation creates rights in many states. Regularising it afterwards is considerably harder than agreeing it first.
  • They want to pay their share to you in cashWithout a record you cannot show the landlord the rent arrived, and you cannot show the roommate it did not.

Frequently asked questions

Is a roommate agreement legally enforceable?

Between the roommates, generally yes: it is an ordinary contract between adults over money and conduct, and a small-claims court will read it. It is not enforceable against the landlord, who never agreed to it. In practice its main value is evidentiary — it settles what everybody agreed to, which is the thing that would otherwise be a matter of competing recollections.

Do all the roommates have to be on the lease?

Not necessarily, and it changes the picture a great deal. A roommate on the lease is the landlord's tenant with full rights and full liability. A roommate not on the lease is usually an occupant with no direct relationship to the landlord — which can mean they have no security of tenure, and also that the landlord cannot pursue them for rent. Know which arrangement you are in before you sign anything.

My roommate stopped paying and the landlord is chasing me. Is that allowed?

If you both signed the lease, yes. Joint and several liability means the landlord can demand the entire rent from whichever tenant is most likely to pay. Pay it to protect your tenancy and your credit, then use the roommate agreement to recover the share from the person who did not. See joint and several liability.

Can we write our own without a lawyer?

Yes. A roommate agreement is not a document that needs legal drafting; it needs specificity. Amounts rather than fractions, dates rather than seasons, names rather than roles. A plainly written page that everybody signs is worth far more than a formal one nobody reads.
