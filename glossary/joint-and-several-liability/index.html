What is joint and several liability in a lease?

Joint and several liability means each tenant who signs is responsible for the whole of the obligation, not their share of it. Three people on a lease do not each owe a third of the rent. Each of them owes all of it, and the landlord may collect from whichever one can pay.

The definition

Joint and several liability is a rule of contract under which each of several parties is independently liable for the entire obligation. In a residential lease signed by more than one tenant, it means the landlord may demand the full rent from any one of them, may sue one without suing the others, and is not obliged to divide the claim.

Nearly every multi-tenant residential lease in the United States contains this clause, usually in a single sentence near the end. It is the clause that most co-tenants have not read and that most co-tenant disputes turn on.

It works in both directions

A breach by one tenant can also be treated as a breach by all — an unauthorised pet or an extra occupant introduced by one roommate is a lease violation the whole tenancy answers for. Liability here is not only about money.

A plain-English example

Three tenants share a house at 2,400 dollars a month and have agreed between themselves to pay 800 dollars each. In March one of them loses their job, pays nothing and stops answering messages.

The landlord is owed 2,400 dollars. They are not owed three separate debts of 800, and they are not required to pursue the one who defaulted. They may demand the entire 2,400 from either of the two who are paying, and in practice they will demand it from whichever looks most likely to pay.

Those two tenants must pay it to protect their tenancy, their credit and their references. What they then have is a claim against the third — and whether that claim is easy or hard depends entirely on whether they ever wrote down what everybody had agreed. That is the whole purpose of a roommate agreement.

The word to notice in your lease

Look for “jointly and severally”. If the lease says the tenants are liable “jointly”, that is a materially different and much rarer arrangement. If it says nothing, most states will read a multi-tenant lease as joint and several by default.

Where it appears, and where it does not

  • It appears in the residential lease and the month-to-month agreement whenever more than one tenant signs.
  • It appears in a sublease, where the original tenant remains liable to the landlord for everything the subtenant does.
  • It does not appear between a landlord and someone who never signed. An adult occupant not named on the lease owes the landlord nothing, which is exactly why landlords insist that every adult signs.
  • It does not bind through a roommate agreement. That document allocates responsibility between the tenants; the landlord is not a party and is not affected by it.

Frequently asked questions

Can I be released from a lease if I move out and the others stay?

Only with the landlord's written release. Moving out does not end your liability — you remain on the lease and remain liable for the rent for the rest of the term. Ask for a written substitution: the new tenant signs, the landlord consents, and you are expressly released. Without that release you are still on the hook whatever anybody says verbally.

Does the landlord have to try the defaulting tenant first?

No. That is precisely what 'several' means: the landlord may pursue any one liable party for the whole amount, in any order, without joining the others. It can feel deeply unfair to the tenant who has paid every month, and it is nonetheless the standard position.

If I pay my roommate's share, can I get it back?

You generally have a claim in contribution against them for the share you paid on their behalf, and a written roommate agreement makes it much easier to prove what their share was. Small claims is the usual venue. The practical difficulty is rarely the legal right; it is finding somebody who has left and collecting from somebody with no money.

Does it apply to the security deposit as well?

The deposit is usually held as one sum for the tenancy and returned as one sum, commonly to one tenant or one address. How it is divided between co-tenants is their own arrangement, which is another thing a roommate agreement should settle at the start rather than at the end.

Can a lease make each tenant liable only for their own share?

Yes, if the landlord agrees to write it that way, and some purpose-built shared housing does exactly that with per-room leases. It is unusual in ordinary house shares because it moves the vacancy risk onto the landlord. If it matters to you, it has to be negotiated before signing — the clause will not be there by default.
