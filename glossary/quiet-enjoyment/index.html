What is the covenant of quiet enjoyment?

Quiet enjoyment is the tenant's right to possess and use the property without substantial interference from the landlord. It is implied in every residential tenancy in the United States whether or not the lease mentions it, and it has almost nothing to do with noise.

The definition

The covenant of quiet enjoyment is an implied promise, in every lease, that the landlord will not substantially interfere with the tenant's use and enjoyment of the property during the tenancy. 'Quiet' here means undisturbed possession, not silence. A tenant who has paid for exclusive possession has bought the right to be left alone in it.

The covenant is implied by law in residential tenancies in every American state. It does not need to be written into the lease, and a clause purporting to waive it is generally unenforceable, in the same way as a waiver of habitability.

The common misreading

People searching this phrase are usually thinking about noisy neighbours. The covenant runs against the landlord and their agents, not against the world. A landlord is generally not responsible for noise from an unrelated neighbour — though they often are where the noise comes from another tenant of the same landlord in the same building, and the landlord has the power to act and does not.

Entry: the rule most disputes are about

Most states permit a landlord to enter for defined purposes — repairs, inspection, showing the property to prospective tenants or buyers, and emergencies — but require advance written notice for everything except the emergency, commonly twenty-four hours, and require entry at reasonable hours.

An emergency means a genuine one: fire, flood, gas, a burst pipe, a credible risk to somebody inside. It does not mean a convenient contractor, and it does not mean suspicion that the tenant has a pet.

A landlord who needs frequent access, during a sale or a renovation, should agree a schedule with the tenant in writing rather than relying on notice each time. It is better for both sides and it is the version that does not end up as a claim.

The lease clause is the practical answer

Write the entry terms into the lease: the notice period, the permitted purposes, the hours, and how notice is given. A tenant who knows the rule rarely objects to entry that follows it, and a landlord who follows it is not guessing about their state's minimum.

Constructive eviction

Where interference becomes severe enough that the property is effectively unusable, the tenant may be able to treat the lease as ended and leave without liability for the remaining rent. That is constructive eviction: the landlord has not formally evicted anybody, but has made the property impossible to live in.

The bar is high and the procedure is strict. Typically the tenant must show a substantial interference caused by the landlord, must have given notice and a reasonable opportunity to fix it, and must actually leave within a reasonable time — a tenant who stays for months is usually taken to have accepted the conditions.

It is a remedy of last resort rather than a first response, and it is the kind of decision worth taking advice on before acting. A tenant who leaves on this basis and is later found to have been wrong owes the rent for the rest of the term.

Frequently asked questions

Can my landlord enter without telling me?

Only in a genuine emergency, in nearly every state. For repairs, inspections or showings, advance written notice is required — commonly twenty-four hours — and entry must be at reasonable hours. A landlord who does it repeatedly is breaching the covenant of quiet enjoyment, and in several states there is a statutory remedy specifically for unlawful entry.

Is quiet enjoyment about noise?

Almost never, despite the name. It protects undisturbed possession. Noise becomes relevant when it is caused by the landlord, or by another tenant of the same landlord who could be dealt with under their own lease and is not. Noise from an unrelated neighbour is a matter for the local noise ordinance rather than the tenancy.

What can I do if my landlord keeps letting themselves in?

Write to them, reference the entry clause and your state's notice requirement, and ask for notice in future — keep a copy. Log each entry with the date and time. If it continues, many states provide a remedy including damages or an injunction, and in some it is grounds for the tenant to terminate. Documented instances are what any of those remedies rest on.

Does the landlord have to fix noisy neighbours?

If the neighbours are their tenants in the same building and their lease has a nuisance clause, a landlord who can act and does nothing may be breaching quiet enjoyment. If the noise is from a property they have nothing to do with, generally not. Report it in writing either way, so there is a record of when they knew.

Can a lease waive the covenant of quiet enjoyment?

Generally not. Like the implied warranty of habitability, it is treated in most states as a protection tenants cannot contract out of, and a clause purporting to waive it is unlikely to be enforced. A lease can and should define the entry terms — that is regulating the covenant, not waiving it.
