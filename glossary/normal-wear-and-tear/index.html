What counts as normal wear and tear?

Normal wear and tear is the deterioration a property suffers from ordinary use over time by a reasonably careful occupant. The landlord pays for it. Damage — anything caused by negligence, accident, misuse or neglect — the tenant pays for. Almost every deposit dispute lives on that line.

The definition

Normal wear and tear is the gradual, expected decline in a property's condition that results from ordinary living: surfaces fading, carpets flattening along walked routes, hinges loosening, paint dulling, grout discolouring. It is not caused by a tenant doing anything wrong, and it is a cost of owning rental property rather than a cost of renting one.

The contrast is with damage: harm caused by an act or an omission, whether deliberate, careless or accidental. A hole is damage. A stain is damage. A dead appliance at the end of its life is wear. The question is never how bad it looks; it is what caused it.

Three tests that usually settle it
  • Cause. Time and ordinary use, or an event? Fading is time. A burn is an event.
  • Avoidability. Could a reasonably careful tenant have prevented it? Traffic wear on a hallway carpet, no. A pet chewing a door frame, yes.
  • Proportion. Is it consistent with the length of the tenancy? Light scuffing after three years is expected. The same after three weeks is not.

Depreciation: why a new carpet is rarely the right number

When a tenant damages something with a finite useful life, many states require the deduction to reflect what was actually lost — the item's remaining life — rather than the cost of a new one. A landlord who charges the full replacement price for a seven-year-old carpet is charging the tenant for an upgrade.

Worked through: a carpet with an assumed ten-year life, installed seven years ago, costing 2,000 dollars to replace. Three years of life remain, so roughly 600 dollars of value was lost. That, plus fitting, is the defensible deduction. The remaining 1,400 dollars was going to be spent anyway.

The same logic applies to paint, appliances and flooring. It is also the reason a landlord should record when major items were installed: a deduction with no installation date behind it cannot be depreciated, and a deduction that cannot be depreciated is frequently reduced to nothing.

What decides the argument in practice

Not the definition. The move-in condition report. The line between wear and damage is applied by comparing the property at the end against the property at the start, and where there is no record of the start, a landlord generally cannot show that the tenant caused anything.

  • A signed, dated, photographed move-in report is the baseline. Without it, most deductions fail.
  • Move-out photographs from the same angles are what a tenant brings to answer them.
  • An invoice or a written estimate supports the amount; a round number invites the question of where it came from.
  • The installation date of the damaged item supports the depreciation.
  • And the lease decides the cleaning question, which is separate from wear and tear entirely.
Cleaning is a different question

Cleaning is not wear and tear and it is not damage. It is a standard the lease sets: the property returned in the condition received, less wear. A tenant who leaves a dirty oven has not damaged it, but may still owe the cleaning — subject to the states that prohibit charging for ordinary turnover cleaning altogether.

Frequently asked questions

Can a landlord charge for repainting?

For fading and light scuffing after a normal tenancy, generally no — paint is wear. For walls a tenant painted without permission, marked heavily, or damaged, generally yes, and in many states depreciated by how old the paint already was. A blanket repainting charge applied to every departing tenant regardless of condition is the version most likely to be struck out.

How many nail holes are too many?

There is no number. Ordinary picture hanging is normally wear; a wall requiring filling and refinishing because of the quantity or the size of the fixings is normally damage. Some leases set the rule expressly — no fixings, or fixings permitted with holes filled on departure — and where they do, the lease governs.

Is a stained carpet always the tenant's responsibility?

Usually the stain is damage, but rarely at the price of a new carpet. Most states expect the deduction to reflect the carpet's remaining useful life. And if the stain sits along an ordinary traffic route on a carpet already near the end of its life, the distinction between staining and wear becomes genuinely arguable.

What if the damage was there when I moved in?

Then it is not yours, and the move-in condition report is what proves it. This is the reason to insist on the walkthrough even when a landlord treats it as a formality — the report protects the tenant at least as much as the landlord. Without one, both sides are arguing from memory and the outcome is unpredictable.

Do I have to professionally clean before I leave?

Only if the lease requires it and your state permits such a clause — several states restrict or prohibit mandatory professional cleaning charges, and others allow them only where the property is actually left below the standard it was received in. Read the clause, and photograph the property clean before you hand back the keys.
