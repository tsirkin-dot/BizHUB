What is a security deposit?

A security deposit is money a tenant gives a landlord at the start of a tenancy to secure the landlord against unpaid rent and damage beyond normal wear and tear. It stays the tenant's money throughout. The landlord holds it; they do not own it.

The definition

A security deposit is a refundable sum paid by a tenant and held by a landlord as security against the tenant's obligations under the lease — principally unpaid rent, damage to the property beyond normal wear and tear, and other costs the lease makes the tenant responsible for. It is refundable by definition: money that is not refundable under any circumstances is a fee, whatever the lease calls it.

In one sentence

The tenant's money, held by the landlord, returnable at the end of the tenancy less whatever the landlord can prove they are owed.

A plain-English example

A tenant pays 1,800 dollars rent and a 1,800 dollar deposit. Over the year the hallway carpet wears along the traffic line and the paint dulls. On the last weekend the tenant's chair leg punches a hole through a bedroom door, and they leave owing 200 dollars of the final month's rent.

At move-out the landlord may deduct the 200 dollars of rent and the cost of repairing the door — the contractor's actual invoice, not a round number. The worn carpet and the dulled paint are normal wear and tear and come out of the landlord's pocket, not the tenant's. The landlord sends an itemised statement listing both deductions with the invoice attached, and returns the balance within the deadline their state sets.

Had the landlord instead written “deposit retained for cleaning and general repairs” and sent nothing else, the deduction would be very hard to sustain, and in many states the failure to itemise within the deadline would forfeit the right to deduct anything at all.

The four rules that attach to a deposit

RuleWhat it usually saysWhere it varies
CapMany states limit the deposit to one or two months' rentSeveral states have no statutory cap; some cap only certain property types; several count last month's rent towards the cap
HoldingThe money is held for the tenant, not spentSeveral states require a separate or escrow account, some require interest to be paid, and many require written notice of where it is held
DeductionUnpaid rent, damage beyond normal wear and tear, and costs the lease provides forSome states prohibit charging for ordinary cleaning; many require depreciation for items with a finite life
ReturnReturned with an itemised statement within a statutory deadlineThe deadline runs from roughly fourteen to sixty days; the penalty for missing it ranges from forfeiting deductions to multiple damages
Calling it something else does not change what it is

In many states a payment that functions as security is a security deposit regardless of its label — “non-refundable deposit”, “move-in fee”, “cleaning deposit”. Some states expressly prohibit non-refundable deposits, and others simply treat the sum as a deposit and apply the cap and the return deadline to it.

Terms it is confused with

TermHow it differs
Last month's rentPaid rent applied to a specific month, not security. In several states it counts towards the deposit cap, and in some it must be held the same way.
Holding depositPaid before a tenancy exists, to take the property off the market. Governed by its own written terms and usually credited or returned at move-in.
Pet depositRefundable security specifically for animal damage. Often counted within the overall cap. Distinct from pet rent, which is rent.
Move-in feeA non-refundable charge for the administrative cost of letting. Permitted in some jurisdictions, prohibited or recharacterised as a deposit in others.
Deposit alternativeAn insurance-style product replacing a deposit for a non-refundable premium. The tenant remains liable for damage; the premium buys nothing back.

Where the term appears in an agreement

  • In the residential lease agreement, stating the amount, what it secures, where it is held and the return process.
  • In the month-to-month rental agreement, on identical terms — the type of tenancy does not change the deposit rules.
  • In a sublease, where a second deposit runs between subtenant and sublandlord and does not touch the original one.
  • In a lease termination agreement, which should say whether the deposit is applied to the final sum or dealt with separately afterwards.
  • In the itemised statement at the end, which is where most of the disputes about it actually happen.

Frequently asked questions

How much can a landlord legally charge for a security deposit?

It depends on the state. Many cap it at one or two months' rent; several have no statutory cap at all; and in some, last month's rent paid in advance counts towards the same ceiling. Where there is no cap, the market sets it, and a deposit noticeably above local norms narrows the applicant pool rather than improving it.

Does a security deposit earn interest?

In several states and a number of cities, yes: the deposit must be held in an interest-bearing account and the interest paid to the tenant annually or at the end of the tenancy. In most states it does not. Where interest is required, failing to pay it is usually a violation in its own right, separate from anything to do with the deposit's return.

Can a landlord use the deposit for unpaid rent during the tenancy?

Generally no, not while the tenancy is running. A deposit secures the landlord's claims at the end of the tenancy; applying it mid-tenancy leaves the landlord unsecured for the remainder and is prohibited in a number of states. At move-out, unpaid rent is a standard permitted deduction.

What happens to the deposit if the property is sold?

The obligation generally transfers to the new owner along with the property, and most states require the deposit itself to be transferred or the tenant to be notified of the transfer. The tenant should get written confirmation of who holds it, because the two parties pointing at each other is the failure mode this rule exists to prevent.

What if I paid a 'non-refundable deposit'?

Look at what the payment actually secures rather than what it is called. In many states a sum held as security for rent or damage is a security deposit regardless of the label, and the cap, the holding rules and the return deadline all apply to it. A genuinely non-refundable fee for a specific service is a different thing, and several states prohibit those in residential tenancies too.
