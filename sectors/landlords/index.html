Renting out property as a private landlord

What a letting year actually looks like

A tenancy is not one event with paperwork attached; it is four phases, and each one has documents that only make sense inside it. Advertising and screening, creating the tenancy, running it, and ending it — in that order, every time.

  1. Before anybody appliesCheck the property is lettable, decide the rent and the term, and find out whether your city requires a licence. See renting out your first property.
  2. Choosing a tenantOne written set of criteria applied to every applicant, with consent for any check. See the rental application.
  3. Creating the tenancyA lease or a month-to-month agreement, plus the condition record on the day the keys move.
  4. Running itRent records, repair records, notice before entry, and the disclosures your state renews rather than gives once.
  5. Changing or ending itA renewal, a termination agreement or a notice to vacate, depending on who wants what.

What the sector gets wrong about scale

A private landlord with three units is not a small property manager. The difference is not volume, it is that nothing is anybody's job: no standard lease, no file that opens itself, no calendar that raises the renewal.

One unit forgives informality because there is only one of everything to remember. Three units is where memory stops working and nothing has yet replaced it, which is why the third tenancy is usually where the first real dispute appears.

The threshold worth watching

When you can no longer say from memory which tenancy is on which version of your lease, the stack has outgrown you rather than the other way round. See the landlord document stack for what to standardise first.

Doing it yourself, or handing it to an agent

The decision is rarely about cost. It is about which of the two failure modes you would rather carry: your own inconsistency, or somebody else acting inside an authority you wrote badly.

What goes wrongDocuments drift, deadlines are missed, records are thinDecisions get made that you did not authorise
What fixes itOne standard set of documents and one place they liveA management agreement that names the limits precisely
Who the tenant's claim lands onYouUsually still you, with the agent behind you

If you do hand it over, the document that decides everything is the management agreement, not the lease. See property management and the sector version at managed portfolios.

Common mistakes

The errors particular to doing this alone, as opposed to the errors anybody makes with a lease.

  • Running each tenancy from memoryWith one unit it works. With three, the renewal date you are sure about is the one you are about to miss, and a missed renewal changes the tenancy on the law's terms rather than yours. See holdover tenant.
  • Treating the deposit as your moneyIt is the tenant's money you are holding. Several states require it held separately, and spending it is a problem that only surfaces on the day you have to return it. See security deposit.
  • Doing repairs without a paper trailThe repair you did promptly and cannot evidence is, in a dispute, the repair you did not do.
  • Being a friend and a landlord at onceInformal arrangements with a tenant you like are the ones that end worst, because nothing was written down while everybody was still reasonable.
  • Learning the law only for the state you live inThe rules that bind you are the ones where the property is, not where you are.

Red flags

Signals from an applicant or a tenant that a private landlord, with no office behind them, should slow down for.

  • An offer to pay well above asking, in cash, immediatelySpeed and cash are usually being exchanged for the absence of a record. See the rental application.
  • A reluctance to have the tenancy in writing at allWhoever proposes the handshake benefits from there being no agreed version of what was agreed.
  • Pressure to skip the move-in recordIt is the one document that protects the tenant as much as you, so resistance to it is worth understanding rather than accommodating. See a new tenant moving in.
  • Requests routed only through a third party you never meetYou are entitled to know who is actually living in the property, and to have every adult named on the agreement.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a written lease if the tenant is family?

An oral tenancy is still a tenancy in most states and still has to be ended lawfully. Writing it down does not add obligations, it records the ones you already have — and it is the family arrangements that most often end in a dispute nobody can evidence.

How many properties before I should incorporate or use an agent?

There is no legal threshold, and the honest trigger is operational rather than numerical: when you can no longer answer from memory which tenancy is on which lease version, and which deposit sits where, the arrangement has outgrown informal management.

What do I have to keep, and for how long?

At minimum the signed agreement, the condition record, every rent receipt, every notice served with proof of service, and the disclosures you gave. Retention periods vary by state, but the practical answer is the length of the tenancy plus the limitation period for a claim on it.

Is a lease from the internet good enough?

It is a starting point, not a finished document. What a generic template cannot carry is your state's mandatory disclosures and its rules on deposits, notice and entry — which is exactly the part that decides disputes.
